# MAPLE: A leakage-audited and explainable framework for real-time multimodal hands-free interaction

**Keywords:** Hands-free human–computer interaction; Data leakage; Model comparison; Cross-subject generalisation; Gaze estimation; Head-pose estimation; Keyword spotting; Explainable AI; Edge inference

## Highlights

- Two models can be compared unfairly even when both pipelines are split correctly.
- Asymmetric exposure is formalised; existing leakage taxonomies omit this category.
- GERA audits a comparison from predictions alone, needing no training data or lineage.
- Membership separates at AUC 0.889; a negative control returns I-squared of zero.
- Correcting the protocol reverses the architectural decision it was meant to inform.

## Abstract

Hands-free interfaces that substitute gaze, blink, head pose and voice for the mouse and keyboard are limited less by their perception components than by how those components are evaluated. We show that group-disjoint partitioning, the standard remedy for identity leakage, repairs each training pipeline in isolation yet leaves the *comparison* between two pipelines contaminated whenever the compared models were developed on overlapping, group-structured corpora and the evaluation sample spans at least one model's partition boundary. We formalise this condition as **asymmetric exposure**, prove that under it the reported contrast between two models is a convex mixture of a memorisation and a generalisation regime whose weight is fixed by a sampling decision rather than by the research question, and show that the established leakage taxonomies contain no category for it because they are organised per pipeline. We introduce **GERA** (Group Error-Ratio Audit), a prediction-only detector requiring neither model's training data — only the two prediction streams and a group label per sample — within **MAPLE**, a framework that also reports each network against the decision rule that consumes it and against attribution on the deployed module. On a four-modality hands-free system, asymmetric exposure inflated one modality's apparent accuracy by 1.905°, of which 0.425°–1.068° (22–56 %) is training familiarity rather than intrinsic difficulty. GERA separates training-partition membership at AUC 0.889 (*p* = 0.006) and returns *I*² = 0.0 % on a negative control against 99.8 % on the contaminated comparison. Under the corrected protocol four networks totalling 7.84 MB run in 6.68 ms per frame on one CPU thread, attaining 93.67 % (blink), 6.439° (gaze), 7.367° (head pose) and 94.53 % (voice). Most consequentially, the correction reverses an architectural decision the uncorrected protocol had supported.

## 1. Introduction

Carpal tunnel syndrome affects between 1 % and 5 % of the general adult population [@cts] and a substantially larger fraction of intensive computer users, and upper-limb motor impairment arising from spinal cord injury, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, cerebral palsy or stroke removes conventional pointing devices from reach altogether. For both populations the operative requirement is the same: the full command surface of a personal computer — cursor positioning, clicking, application and window switching, text entry, scrolling — must be reachable without the hands. Systems that meet this requirement by substituting *gaze* for the cursor, *blink* for the click, *head pose* for discrete gestures and *voice* for command entry have been demonstrated repeatedly in the literature, yet deployment outside the laboratory remains rare. The obstacles are not principally in the individual perception components, each of which has strong published solutions; they lie in the joins between them, and in how the evidence for them is produced.

Three obstacles recur across the literature and are the organising concerns of this work.

- **Evaluation that does not survive an unseen user.** Appearance-based perception corpora are group-structured: the MRL Eye Dataset [@mrl] holds roughly 2,300 frames of the same eye per subject, MPIIGaze [@mpiigaze][@mpiigaze_pami] several thousand normalised patches per participant, and 300W-LP [@w300lp] synthesises a median of 34 viewpoint renders of each of its 3,837 base identities. Partitioning such corpora by random row sampling places near-duplicate observations of one individual on both sides of the split, reducing the test task from generalisation to nearest-neighbour retrieval against an almost identical stored exemplar. The remedy — group-disjoint partitioning keyed on subject, participant or face identity — is standard and well documented [@leakage][@kapoor]. What is not standard, and what this paper argues is the more consequential omission, is that applying the remedy correctly to *every model in a comparison* does not make the comparison correct. When a proposed model and its pretrained baseline were developed on overlapping corpora, the evaluation sample can lie inside one model's training partition and outside the other's, and the resulting contrast measures exposure asymmetry rather than capability. Nothing in the per-pipeline audits prescribed by the standard taxonomies [@kapoor][@leaktypes] detects this, because each such audit sees only one pipeline and neither pipeline carries any representation of the other's partition boundary.

- **A budget that must accommodate concurrency.** A hands-free interface is not one perception problem but four, running simultaneously, on the machine the user already owns, without an accelerator. The camera loop at 30 frames per second affords a 33 ms budget within which face landmarking, three vision networks, two finite-state machines and a cursor stabilisation chain must all complete; a fourth modality runs on a separate audio thread. Accuracy obtained at a cost that forecloses concurrency is not usable accuracy, so the operative question is not which model is most accurate but which accuracy–footprint operating points admit four simultaneous modalities.

- **Component metrics that do not predict composite behaviour.** No perception network in such a system is read directly. Each feeds a decision rule with its own thresholds, dwell times, hysteresis bands and filters, and it is that composite the user experiences. A per-frame blink classification is not a click; a click is a closure sustained past 420 ms, roughly thirteen consecutive frames, with natural blinks of 100–300 ms rejected by construction. A head-pose estimate is not a gesture; a gesture is a threshold crossing held for 650 ms inside an 18° hysteresis band. A raw gaze angle is not a cursor position; it is the input to a per-user polynomial calibration corrected online by recursive least squares [@rls] and smoothed by a one-euro filter [@onceuro]. Reporting the component metric and reasoning about the composite as though the two were interchangeable inverts several conclusions, as Section 5 demonstrates in three separate places.

These three obstacles are treated in isolation in the existing literature. Work that strengthens evaluation protocol rarely reports deployment cost; work that optimises for edge inference rarely audits its splits; work that adds interpretability rarely connects the explanation to the decision rule that consumes the model's output. The fragmentation, rather than any single gap, is what keeps demonstrated hands-free systems in the laboratory.

### 1.1 Contributions

This paper presents MAPLE (Multimodal Audited Perception with Leakage-aware Evaluation), a framework that treats the three obstacles as one problem, and reports the evaluation of a four-modality hands-free interaction system conducted under it. The contributions are as follows.

1. **Formalisation of asymmetric exposure as a distinct leakage class.** We define a cross-model comparison as *asymmetrically exposed* when three conditions hold simultaneously: the compared models were developed on overlapping corpora; the corpus carries group structure with strong within-group dependence; and the evaluation sample is drawn from groups lying on both sides of at least one compared model's training-partition boundary. We prove (Theorem 1) that under these conditions the affected model's aggregate score is a convex combination of a memorisation regime and a generalisation regime whose mixing weight is fixed by a sampling quota, so that the reported accuracy can be changed without changing any model, any datum or any metric definition. We show that neither of the two standard leakage taxonomies [@kapoor][@leaktypes] contains a category for this fault, and that the existing remedies — lineage tracing [@tsfm] and contamination search [@oren] — presume documentation or corpus access an external auditor rarely has.

2. **GERA: a prediction-only detector with a negative control.** We introduce the Group Error-Ratio Audit, a statistic *r*(*g*) formed as the ratio of an audited model's per-group mean error to a reference model's mean error on the same group. We prove (Proposition 2) that under a multiplicative difficulty model the intrinsic difficulty term cancels exactly, leaving a quantity that varies across groups only through differential familiarity. GERA adapts difficulty calibration — developed for strengthening membership inference attacks [@shokri][@watson] — from example level to group level, which is the shift that makes it stable enough to read off published predictions. It requires only the two prediction streams over a shared evaluation set and a group label per sample: no training data, no lineage documentation, no corpus access. On the system studied here it separates training-partition membership at AUC 0.889 with Mann–Whitney *p* = 0.006, and a negative control on a modality where neither model saw the evaluation groups returns *I*² = 0.0 % against 99.8 % for the contaminated comparison, establishing that it stays silent when it should even under a fourfold swing in intrinsic difficulty.

3. **Decomposition of the exposure bias into difficulty and familiarity.** We show (Theorem 3) that the same reference model that calibrates the detector also decomposes the observed inflation into an intrinsic-difficulty and a familiarity component. The split depends on a transfer coefficient the data do not identify, so we report it as an interval rather than a point estimate: of a 1.905° aggregate inflation on the affected modality, the familiarity component is 0.425°–1.068° (22 %–56 %), the ends corresponding to a multiplicative and an additive difficulty model respectively. Under both, intrinsic difficulty fails to account for the whole gap. This is a quantity lineage analysis cannot supply at all, since knowing *that* a model saw a corpus does not say how much of its advantage came from having seen it.

4. **A six-step corrected comparison protocol (EDCP), and its exercise on four modalities.** We state an Exposure-Disjoint Comparison Protocol whose first two steps are established practice, whose steps three through five formalise for group-structured corpora the discipline that lineage analysis prescribes informally, and whose sixth step adds the prediction-only audit. We then exercise the protocol on four perception modalities spanning two regression and two classification tasks, reporting for each the published score under the faulty protocol, a fair baseline retraining the original hyperparameters under the corrected protocol, and a tuned score under that same protocol, so that the protocol effect and the optimisation effect can never be conflated.

5. **Four compact task-specific networks characterised at their deployed operating point.** We design, tune and evaluate four convolutional networks trained from scratch — not fine-tuned backbones — spanning 101,766 to 1,142,339 parameters and 7.84 MB in aggregate, each deployed as a self-contained TorchScript module [@torchscript][@pytorch] running single-threaded on CPU, so that every latency and accuracy figure is measured on the artefact that ships rather than on a research-time proxy. Aggregate vision-pipeline cost is 6.68 ms per frame inside a 33 ms budget, which is the condition that permits four modalities to run concurrently without an accelerator.

6. **A unified mechanistic account of the residual error.** We show that a single parameter — the fitted regression gain of predicted on true target — governs the error structure of both regression modalities, accounting for the isotropic radial growth of gaze error, the constant-ratio degradation of head-pose error across the pose manifold, the axis-ranking inversions between absolute and variance-normalised metrics, and the absence of any localised failure mode. We further show that inverting the fitted gain *increases* angular error by 28.5 %, establishing that the contraction is the estimator's variance-optimal response to representational uncertainty rather than a defect, and thereby redirecting engineering effort from calibration to representation. For the two classification modalities we show the opposite diagnosis: ranking quality far exceeds operating-point accuracy, placing their residual error in decision logic, which is why a confidence gate and a threshold move improve them where additional capacity would not.

7. **Decision-rule-aware reporting, which inverts three conclusions.** By stating for each modality the deployed decision rule and the error statistic that rule makes diagnostic, we obtain three conclusions the component metrics alone would contradict: head pose survives an 8.74° yaw error because its error is unbiased and its gesture rule averages roughly twenty frames inside an 18° hysteresis band; blink inverts the conventional cost asymmetry, so thresholds *below* the default are defensible because an isolated false positive cannot fire a click whereas a false negative breaks a deliberate closure run; and the weakest keyword in the voice vocabulary is bound to the only command that reactivates a paused interface, making the largest error mode a vocabulary-design fault removable at zero computational cost.

8. **A demonstration that the protocol fault is consequential rather than pedantic.** Under the uncorrected protocol the proposed gaze network scored 2.84°, level with its pretrained baseline's all-participant figure of 2.835°, and the decision was to retain it. Under the corrected protocol the true held-out contrast is 6.439° against 3.621°, the baseline is simultaneously 7.1× smaller, and the recommendation reverses to adopting the pretrained model. We know of no sharper available demonstration that the distinction between a per-pipeline audit and a per-comparison audit changes engineering outcomes.

## 2. Related work

Hands-free interaction, evaluation integrity and efficient inference have largely separate literatures, and the framework proposed here sits at their intersection. This section reviews each along the dimension that corresponds to one of the three obstacles of Section 1: perception modalities and their cross-subject generalisation behaviour (Section 2.1), evaluation integrity and the leakage taxonomies (Section 2.2), and efficiency and interpretability for deployed perception (Section 2.3). Section 2.4 draws the cross-challenge synthesis and states the gaps that motivate MAPLE.

Throughout, we adopt the convention that a *challenge* is numbered as in Section 1: **C1** evaluation integrity under group structure and cross-model comparison, **C2** concurrent real-time operation within a commodity CPU budget, **C3** mechanistic interpretability tied to the deployed decision rule.

### 2.1 Perception modalities for hands-free interaction (C2, C3)

#### 2.1.1 Appearance-based gaze estimation

Gaze estimation for interaction [@majaranta] divides into model-based methods, which fit a geometric eye model and recover the optical axis [@morimoto], and appearance-based methods, which regress gaze direction directly from image intensities. Model-based approaches are accurate under controlled illumination and with high-resolution imagery, but degrade sharply on the consumer webcams that a deployable assistive system must assume. Appearance-based estimation on MPIIGaze established the modern benchmark [@mpiigaze][@mpiigaze_pami]: the corpus provides 213,659 images from fifteen participants captured over months of everyday laptop use, and the accompanying normalisation procedure — warping each eye patch to a canonical pose and scale — is what makes cross-participant learning tractable at all. Reported person-independent angular errors on MPIIGaze under leave-one-person-out evaluation have improved from roughly 6° for early convolutional regressors to under 4° for residual architectures [@resnet][@preact] with head-pose conditioning.

Two properties of this literature matter for the present work. First, the standard protocol is leave-one-person-out over all fifteen participants, which is the correct group-disjoint design; deviations from it — including the single group-disjoint split adopted here under compute constraint — weaken the cross-person estimate and must be declared. Second, and more consequentially for Section 3.5, the strongest publicly available MPIIGaze baselines are trained on all fifteen participants. Any comparison of a newly trained network against such a baseline, evaluated on a sample drawn from those fifteen, is asymmetrically exposed by construction: the baseline has seen every evaluation group and the proposed network has not.

A second and largely separate concern is the gap between the normalised patches on which appearance-based estimators are trained and the landmark-cropped regions a live pipeline delivers. This is a deterministic covariate shift rather than sampling noise, and Section 5.2 quantifies it and shows that domain-targeted augmentation removes part of it.

#### 2.1.2 Blink detection and intentional-blink interfaces

Blink detection for interaction faces a problem absent from blink detection for drowsiness monitoring: the interface must distinguish a *deliberate* closure, intended as a click, from the involuntary blinks that occur roughly fifteen to twenty times per minute. The dominant geometric approach computes an eye-aspect ratio from six landmarks per eye and thresholds it [@ear], which is inexpensive and requires no training but presumes reliable full-face landmarking and is sensitive to eyewear, illumination and head pose. Learned alternatives operate on cropped eye regions and are correspondingly robust to the landmarking failures that break the geometric rule, at the cost of requiring a labelled corpus. The MRL Eye Dataset [@mrl], roughly 85,000 infrared and greyscale eye crops from 37 subjects annotated for open/closed state along with glasses, illumination and sensor attributes, is the standard resource.

The interface-level literature converges on duration coding: a closure sustained past a threshold selects an action, with distinct thresholds for left click, right click and double click, and a refractory period preventing repeat fires. This construction has an evaluation consequence that Section 5.5 develops at length and that we have not seen stated in the blink-detection literature: because a click requires a run of consecutive above-threshold frames, an isolated per-frame false positive cannot fire one, whereas a per-frame false negative breaks a run in progress and silently cancels a deliberate click. The frame-level cost asymmetry that the confusion matrix suggests is therefore inverted by the decision rule, and the accuracy-optimal threshold is also the run-integrity-optimal threshold.

#### 2.1.3 Head-pose estimation and gesture triggering

Head-pose estimation from a single image is posed either as landmark-plus-PnP — solve a perspective-n-point problem against a canonical 3D face model — or as direct regression of Euler angles [@ruiz] or a continuous rotation representation [@rot6d]. The landmark route inherits the landmark detector's failure modes and suffers a well-known sign ambiguity in yaw under weak perspective; the direct route requires a corpus spanning the pose manifold. 300W-LP [@w300lp], which synthesises large-pose views from 300-W faces via a 3D morphable model, and AFLW2000 [@aflw], its untouched 2,000-image companion, form the standard train/test pairing, and this pairing is disjoint by construction — a property that Section 3.5 exploits as a negative control for the leakage detector, since it guarantees that neither a proposed network nor a pretrained baseline trained on 300W-LP has seen an AFLW2000 identity.

The strongest current methods parameterise rotation continuously rather than as Euler angles [@sixdrep][@rot6d], avoiding the discontinuity that Euler representations introduce and that harms gradient-based learning near gimbal configurations. Reported mean absolute errors on AFLW2000 have fallen below 4° for large networks. As with gaze, the interface consumes not the estimate but a thresholded state machine over it, and the relevant statistic is consequently per-axis *bias* — which survives dwell averaging — rather than variance, which does not.

#### 2.1.4 Small-footprint keyword spotting

Keyword spotting for command interfaces is the one modality where compact purpose-built models are already the norm rather than the exception, because the task is a closed-vocabulary classification over one-second clips rather than open transcription. Google Speech Commands v2 [@speechcmd], comprising 105,829 utterances of 35 words from 2,618 speakers with an official speaker-disjoint train/validation/test partition, is the standard benchmark. Convolutional classifiers over log-mel spectrograms [@mfcc][@sainath] reach competitive accuracy at parameter counts three orders of magnitude below general-purpose automatic speech recognition engines.

The comparison against a general ASR engine [@vosk][@kaldi] is not like-for-like in a way that matters for interface design and that Section 5.4 quantifies. A production ASR engine equipped with a rejection option converts would-be substitutions into non-responses, which macro-averaged F1 rewards — abstention costs recall but protects precision — whereas a forced-choice classifier must emit a hypothesis for every input. In an interface, a substitution executes an unintended command while an abstention costs one repetition, so the two error types are not interchangeable and the fair comparison is a risk–coverage curve rather than a single accuracy figure.

#### 2.1.5 Cross-subject generalisation: a common pattern

Across all four modalities the same quantitative pattern recurs and is well documented in the adjacent physiological-signal literature [@lotte]: within-subject or randomly partitioned evaluation reports accuracy in a high band, while subject-independent protocols — leave-one-subject-out, leave-one-person-out, or any group-disjoint split — yield materially lower figures, with degradations of ten to twenty percentage points routinely reported. The degradation reflects genuine inter-individual variability in anatomy, appearance and expression dynamics rather than a defect of the stricter protocol. For an assistive interface intended for a user the system has never seen, the subject-independent figure is the only deployment-relevant one, and Table 1 accordingly records the grouping key for every corpus used in this work.

### 2.2 Evaluation integrity and data leakage (C1)

#### 2.2.1 Leakage taxonomies and per-pipeline auditing

Leakage — the presence in the training data of information about the evaluation target that will be unavailable at deployment — is a mature topic with an established formulation and a well-known catalogue of instances [@leakage][@kohavi]. The canonical treatment [@leakage] distinguishes leakage in feature construction from leakage in the training-set design, and identifies the case relevant here as arising from non-independent records: when observations are grouped and the grouping is ignored by the partitioning strategy, the split does not match the deployment boundary. Subsequent surveys have catalogued the resulting reproducibility failures across many scientific fields, enumerating eight types organised by where in a single pipeline the contamination enters [@kapoor], and a complementary quantitative landscape organises the same phenomena into four causal classes [@leaktypes].

Both taxonomies share a structural property that is decisive for this paper: they are organised *per pipeline*. Every type they enumerate is a relation between one model's training data and its own evaluation data, and every audit they prescribe operates on one pipeline at a time. Neither contains a category for a fault whose two terms live in different pipelines, and one of the two explicitly identifies the possibility of pathways outside its enumerated classes. Section 3.5 argues that asymmetric exposure between compared models is exactly such a pathway, and that it is undetectable by the audits these taxonomies prescribe, since neither pipeline carries any representation of the other model's partition boundary.

#### 2.2.2 Contamination, foundation models and asymmetric exposure

The phenomenon this paper formalises has been observed, though not under this name and not in this domain. In time-series forecasting, models pretrained on large public corpora are routinely benchmarked on datasets those corpora contain, and a controlled study [@tsfm] measures 47 %–184 % lower error for exposed models against unexposed competitors — the same direction as, and a larger magnitude than, the effect we report. The parallel literature on benchmark contamination in language models addresses the same underlying failure from the corpus side, and provides a black-box statistical test with a false-positive guarantee based on the exchangeability of benchmark example ordering [@oren].

The remedies these literatures propose require what an auditor usually lacks. Lineage analysis — tracing which datasets entered each model's pretraining — presumes that lineage is documented and truthful. Contamination search presumes access to the pretraining corpus. Both are unavailable when auditing a published comparison against a third-party pretrained model whose training partition is undocumented and whose corpus is not distributed, which is the ordinary case in applied computer vision and the case the present work addresses. The contribution here is therefore not to identify the hazard but to make it *auditable* under the access constraints an external auditor actually faces.

#### 2.2.3 Membership inference and difficulty calibration

The mechanism GERA repurposes originates in the privacy literature. Membership inference attacks [@shokri] determine whether a particular record was in a model's training set, and the earliest formulations suffer a systematic bias: intrinsically easy or over-represented records produce low loss regardless of membership, inflating false positives. The standard correction [@watson] calibrates a per-example membership score by the discrepancy between the target model and a *reference* model trained without the record, so that intrinsic difficulty is priced out and only the membership-attributable component remains.

GERA applies that same calibration at *group* rather than example level, and repurposes it from attacking a model's privacy to auditing a comparison's validity. The shift in granularity is what makes it practical for the present problem: group-level means are stable where per-example scores are noisy, group labels are published with most corpora, and the quantity of interest — whether a *stratum* was seen — is a group property rather than an example property. Section 3.5.3 develops the statistic and Proposition 2 states the condition under which the intrinsic difficulty term cancels exactly.

### 2.3 Efficient and interpretable on-device perception (C2, C3)

#### 2.3.1 Compact architectures and the concurrency constraint

The efficient-inference literature is large and mostly concerned with a single model's accuracy–cost frontier: depthwise-separable convolutions and channel scaling [@mobilenet], quantisation, pruning and distillation [@distill] all reduce the cost of one network. The constraint operative here is different in kind. Four perception problems must be solved concurrently within one frame period on a CPU shared with the application the user is actually driving, so the binding quantity is the *sum* of per-model costs plus the shared front end, and the design question is which accuracy an individual model may sacrifice in order that the sum fits.

This changes what counts as a good result. A compact model is Pareto-relevant only if its accuracy penalty is sub-linear in its cost reduction by a wide margin; a model that is 34× smaller and 2.1× less accurate occupies a defensible frontier position, whereas a model that is larger *and* less accurate than an available alternative is dominated and no operating point rescues it. Section 5.7 reports one instance of each outcome among the four modalities studied, and reports them separately rather than averaging them into a single verdict.

A second consideration specific to CPU inference is that at these model sizes inference is bound by memory traffic rather than by arithmetic, so total resident footprint — and in particular whether the working set stays in last-level cache — matters disproportionately relative to nominal floating-point operation counts. Section 3.12 formalises the cost model accordingly.

#### 2.3.2 Attribution methods and the axioms that make them auditable

Post-hoc attribution assigns to each input component a share of a model's output. Gradient-based saliency [@simonyan] is inexpensive but suffers saturation; perturbation and additive-attribution methods [@lundberg] are model-agnostic but expensive and sensitive to the choice of reference distribution. Integrated Gradients [@ig] occupies a distinctive position for the present purpose because it satisfies *completeness*: the attributions sum exactly to the difference between the model's output at the input and at a chosen baseline. Completeness is what converts an attribution plot from an illustration into an audit, since the decomposition is exact rather than indicative. Integrated Gradients is furthermore the Aumann–Shapley [@aumann] continuous analogue of the Shapley value [@shapley], inheriting a cooperative-game-theoretic justification for the allocation.

For the present work three further properties matter. Attribution is computed directly on the deployed TorchScript modules [@torchscript], so what is explained is what executes rather than a research-time reimplementation. Because the networks consume images and spectrograms rather than named columns, the input is partitioned into a regular grid and each region assumes the role of a feature, which makes the decomposition interpretable anatomically. And the same four views — global summary, per-instance waterfall, similarity-ordered global explanation, and dataset-ordered global explanation — are reported for every modality, so that the presence or absence of structure under each ordering is comparable across tasks. The dataset-ordered view in particular is a direct test of subject-invariance whenever the corpus is ordered by subject, and Section 5.5 uses it exactly so.

#### 2.3.3 Interpretability in hands-free and assistive systems

Explainability in assistive perception remains exceptional rather than standard. Where it appears, it is typically applied to a single architecture and reported as qualitative evidence that the model attends to plausible regions, without integration with the evaluation protocol or with the decision rule that consumes the output. The framework proposed here treats attribution as a third leg of the evidence rather than as illustration: the mechanism identified for each modality must be consistent with the error structure measured for it, and where the two disagree the disagreement is reported. Section 5.2 gives an instance in which attribution independently explains an axis asymmetry that the error analysis identifies but cannot account for.

Three observations follow.

**On C1.** Every reviewed perception study that partitions honestly does so within its own pipeline, and none audits the comparison against its baseline for exposure asymmetry. The methodological literature that does address cross-model contamination requires access — lineage documentation or the pretraining corpus — that is unavailable in the ordinary applied case. The gap is therefore not that the hazard is unknown but that no instrument exists for the access regime in which most comparisons are actually made.

**On C2.** Deployment cost is reported inconsistently and almost never for a *set* of models required to run concurrently. Where latency appears it is typically a single model on a research configuration rather than the shipped artefact, and aggregate budgets are not stated, so the question of whether a reported accuracy is achievable simultaneously with the rest of a system cannot be answered from the literature.

**On C3.** Interpretability, where present, is decoupled from both the evaluation protocol and the decision rule. No reviewed work connects an attribution result to the threshold, dwell or filter that consumes the model's output, which is the connection that determines whether a mechanistic finding has an engineering consequence.

MAPLE is designed to close all three simultaneously, and the remainder of the paper describes it and reports its exercise.
